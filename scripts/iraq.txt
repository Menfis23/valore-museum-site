Iraq Gallery.

The birthplace of writing and the Code of Hammurabi, Baghdad's medieval House of Wisdom, and the interpreters who became American allies.

Writing Began Here.

More than five thousand years ago, the Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia — the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers that gives Iraq its ancient name — invented cuneiform, the earliest writing system known to survive, pressing wedge-shaped marks into wet clay around 3400 BCE. The same region produced the Code of Hammurabi, one of the first written legal codes, carved into a stone stele around 1754 BCE, and the Epic of Gilgamesh, widely considered the oldest surviving work of literature in the world. The Great Ziggurat of Ur, a massive stepped temple built around 2100 BCE, still stands today — a monument to a civilization that gave the world not just its first stories and laws, but the concepts of the 60-minute hour and the 360-degree circle.

A Golden Age of Ideas.

In the 9th century, the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun turned Baghdad's House of Wisdom into one of history's great centers of learning, where scholars translated Greek, Persian, and Indian texts and built on them — the mathematician al-Khwarizmi worked there around 820 CE, and his book on solving equations gave the world the word "algebra" (from the Arabic al-jabr) and, from his own name, the word "algorithm." That same spirit of hospitality and intellectual exchange lives on in the diwan, a dedicated sitting room where guests are received with tea and conversation, sometimes for hours, as a matter of course. Iraqi maqam, a highly structured classical vocal tradition accompanied by instruments like the oud and santur, has been passed from teacher to student for generations, and family tables are built around dishes like masgouf — carp grilled whole over an open fire — and dolma, vegetables and grape leaves stuffed and simmered together.

Beyond the Headlines.

Iraq's population reflects real diversity beyond a single story: Arab and Kurdish communities make up the majority, alongside Assyrian and Chaldean Christians, Yazidis, and other groups whose presence in the region predates Islam by centuries. The last five decades brought real hardship — Saddam Hussein's dictatorship, the Iran-Iraq War of the 1980s, the 2003 US-led invasion, and the rise of ISIS — and hundreds of thousands of Iraqis, including many who served as interpreters and support staff for American forces and diplomats, were resettled in the US through the Special Immigrant Visa program, with families rebuilding lives in cities including Pittsburgh. That recent history sits alongside a much older one: a civilization that gave the world its first writing, its first laws, and, at the House of Wisdom, some of its first algebra — a depth of history that Iraqi-American families carry forward regardless of which chapter dominates the headlines.
