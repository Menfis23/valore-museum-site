Bahaty Gallery.

A painter reimagining classical portraiture around Black womanhood, and carrying the Democratic Republic of Congo's flag into scenes of family, dance, and quiet resistance.

A New Room in the Gallery.

Bahaty, who signs her canvases BHTart, paints her way into art history rather than around it — her reimagining of Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring keeps the composition's every hallmark, the turned head, the parted lips, the improbable earring catching the light, and recasts its subject as a young Black woman, gold hoop through her nose instead of pearl in her ear. It's less a copy than a claim: the same technical control the old masters are praised for, turned toward a face the canon rarely painted with that much care. This room gathers eight of her paintings, spanning that kind of art-historical conversation, scenes of Congolese heritage carried through family and dance, and quieter, more interior work about calm, power, and time.

Congo, Remembered and Reimagined.

The flag of the Democratic Republic of Congo, that diagonal red-and-yellow stripe across blue, recurs across several canvases here like a signature the paintings themselves carry. In one, a mother braids her daughter's hair beneath it, an unhurried, everyday scene of care and inheritance rendered in the same register as a family photograph. In another, two dancers in traditional dress move against a burning orange sunset, the flag draped behind them like a backdrop at a homecoming. A third painting, titled Equity, is harder-edged: a woman with a hand clamped over her mouth, the flag stitched into her clothing, sharing the canvas with a mother cradling her child and a set of scales hanging in the sky above the word itself. Read together, the three paintings hold both halves of the same inheritance, the tenderness and the injustice, without letting either one cancel the other out.

Inner Worlds.

Not every piece in this room points outward. In one portrait, a woman's headwrap and hair are painted in the exact silhouette of the African continent, filled with a miniature sunset and treeline, as if the shape of home were something she carried on her head rather than a place she'd left behind. Inner Calm shows a young woman crowned mid-meditation, hands not her own settling the tiara into place above closed eyes, the title's promise, find the power within you, doubling as instruction for how to look at the painting. Strings of Time takes a darker turn: a disembodied hand suspends a ringing alarm clock on threads tied to its own fingers, equal parts marionette and warning about who's really setting the pace of your day. And in Sisters, two women dissolve into laughter, one leaning into the other's shoulder, camera-real and unguarded in a way the more formal portraits deliberately aren't.
