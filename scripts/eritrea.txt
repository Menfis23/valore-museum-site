Piccola Roma and an Ancient Red Sea Port.

Long before Italian colonial rule left its mark, this stretch of the Red Sea coast was already a crossroads of ancient trade: the port of Adulis linked the highlands of the Horn of Africa to Egypt, Arabia, India, and the wider Mediterranean world for centuries, exporting ivory, tortoiseshell, and obsidian until a flood and earthquake destroyed it in the 7th or 8th century. Centuries later, Italian colonization from 1890 to 1941 left an unexpected legacy in the capital: architects built roughly 400 Art Deco and Futurist buildings across Asmara, earning it the nickname "La Piccola Roma," Little Rome. Landmarks like the swooping, airplane-winged Fiat Tagliero service station and the 1937 Cinema Impero are so well preserved that UNESCO named Asmara a World Heritage Site in 2017 — one of the world's most complete assemblages of Modernist architecture, in an African capital most people have never pictured that way.

Coffee, Injera, and Nine Peoples.

Coffee, or buna, is at the center of Eritrean social life — beans are roasted, ground, and brewed by hand in a clay jebena pot with a single spout, then served in three ceremonial rounds, awel, kalai, and bereka, each considered progressively stronger and more blessed, over a ritual that can stretch for hours among family and neighbors. Meals center on injera, a spongy sourdough flatbread torn by hand and used to scoop up tsebhi stews of meat, lentils, or vegetables shared from one large plate. Eritrea's nine recognized ethnic groups, including the Tigrinya, Tigre, Afar, and Saho, each carry their own dress, music, and language, and Eritrean popular music blends traditional string instruments like the krar with guitar-driven, often politically minded songwriting.

Beyond the Headlines.

Eritrea fought a 30-year war of independence from Ethiopia, finally winning it in 1991 and formalizing statehood in 1993 — one of the longest armed independence struggles in modern African history. A brutal 1998–2000 border war with Ethiopia left the two countries in unresolved tension for two decades, and the Eritrean government's policy of indefinite, open-ended national service — technically 18 months but in practice often extended for years — has driven large numbers of young Eritreans to leave the country, many passing through refugee camps in Ethiopia or Sudan or undertaking dangerous journeys toward Europe before resettling in cities including Pittsburgh. That hardship sits alongside a much older story: a Red Sea trading history, an architectural heritage unlike anywhere else in Africa, and a coffee ceremony that turns something as small as a cup of coffee into hours of deliberate, unhurried togetherness.
