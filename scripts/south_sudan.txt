South Sudan Gallery.

Cattle-camp songs, intricate beadwork, and a hard-won 2011 independence carried by dozens of distinct peoples.

Cattle, Song, and a Nation of Many Peoples.

South Sudan is home to dozens of distinct ethnic groups — Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Azande, Bari, Mundari, and many more — each with its own language and pastoral or agricultural traditions that predate any national border. For Dinka and Nuer cattle-keepers, a family's cattle are not simply livestock but the center of an entire social and spiritual world: each young man takes a name from his favorite ox and composes praise songs for it, and cattle camps along the Nile's swamplands become, for months each dry season, the true center of communal life — courtship, music, and the shaping of character all happen there.

Everyday Life & Art.

Dinka men and women once wore elaborate beaded corsets and aprons — some taking months to complete — whose colors and patterns marked age, clan, and marital status as precisely as a written record. Singing runs through daily life: ox-songs called kêêp praise a favorite bull by name, while young people at seasonal cattle camps compose waak, courtship songs performed late into the night. Wrestling, practiced across Dinka, Mundari, and other communities, is both a rite of passage for young men and a form of public art, drawing crowds for matches that double as dance and music — and has increasingly become a deliberate tool for building peace between rival communities.

Beyond the Headlines.

After Africa's longest civil war — more than two decades fighting for self-rule from Sudan — South Sudanese voters went to the polls in January 2011 and chose independence by a nearly unanimous 99%; the Republic of South Sudan was born that July, a moment of genuine triumph after generations of struggle. That joy was shadowed just two years later when a political rift exploded into a new civil war in 2013, driven partly by rivalry between the Dinka and Nuer communities, displacing millions more. Long before either war, waves of unaccompanied children — the "Lost Boys and Girls of Sudan" — walked hundreds of miles to refugee camps in Ethiopia and Kenya, and many were resettled in the U.S., including Pittsburgh, in the late 1990s and 2000s; that same courage and resilience continues in the South Sudanese families rebuilding community here today.
