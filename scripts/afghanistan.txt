A Silk Road crossroads of poets and rug-weavers, and the community that arrived after 2021.

A Crossroads of Empires.

For more than two thousand years, Afghanistan sat at the crossroads of the Silk Road, where Persian, Greek, Central Asian, and South Asian trade routes met and empires from Alexander the Great to the Mongols passed through and left their mark. In the central highlands, the Bamiyan valley once held two colossal 6th-century Buddha statues carved into sandstone cliffs — among the largest standing Buddhas ever built — until the Taliban destroyed them in 2001, an act that shocked the world and erased 1,500 years of continuous history in an instant.

Poetry, Rugs, and a Nation of Poets.

Few countries hold poetry in higher regard: Dari-language poets like Rumi, born in what is now Afghanistan, and the 10th-century poet Rabi'a Balkhi, one of the first women to write poetry in New Persian, are still recited from memory at Afghan gatherings today. Hand-knotted Afghan rugs — Turkmen, Baluch, and Uzbek weaving traditions among them — are prized worldwide for their density and detail, and Afghan music blends Indian classical forms with Persian melody on instruments like the rubab, considered an ancestor of the sitar. Family and hospitality carry deep weight: guests are served tea and treated with mehman-nawazi, a code of generosity outsiders often describe as instantly, almost overwhelmingly warm.

Beyond the Headlines.

For many Americans, Afghanistan is shorthand for two decades of war — but that war followed a Soviet invasion in 1979 and preceded further conflict, uprooting generation after generation. When the Afghan government fell in August 2021, the U.S. carried out one of the largest airlifts in its history, evacuating more than 124,000 people in weeks; in total, roughly 190,000 Afghans were resettled to the United States, many of them interpreters, engineers, and government workers who had risked their lives working alongside American forces. Afghan families rebuilding life in cities like Pittsburgh carry a culture far older, and far richer, than the war that brought them here.
