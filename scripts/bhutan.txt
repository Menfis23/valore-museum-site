Bhutan Gallery.

Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa families, Himalayan textiles, and two decades of statelessness before finding home.

Between Two Homes.

Southern Bhutan has been home to ethnic Nepali-speaking communities — the Lhotshampa, "people of the south" — for generations, farmers who settled the Himalayan foothills starting in the late 1800s. In the late 1980s, Bhutan's government began requiring a single national dress and language and reclassified many longtime Lhotshampa residents as illegal immigrants. Between 1990 and 1993, more than 100,000 people were forced from their homes — through arrests, intimidation, and coerced "voluntary migration" paperwork — and fled into seven UNHCR camps in eastern Nepal, where most would spend the next fifteen to twenty years.

Faith, Cloth, and Cuisine.

While Bhutan's dominant Drukpa culture is Vajrayana Buddhist, most Lhotshampa are Hindu, and inside the Nepal camps families kept Hindu festivals like Dashain and Tihar alive alongside their Nepali language, passing down devotional songs, dal bhat, and momos across two decades of displacement. Bhutan's broader textile tradition is still renowned — the hand-woven kira worn by women and gho worn by men, and intricate raw-silk kishuthara cloth from Lhuentse, take weavers months to complete on backstrap looms. Even in the camps, women kept weaving and stitching, teaching daughters skills that had nothing to do with the land that had just expelled them.

A Community Built From Waiting.

For nearly two decades, the Lhotshampa lived in political limbo — stateless in Nepal, unwelcome in Bhutan, waiting for a solution that never fully came. In 2007, a third-country resettlement program finally opened, and more than 113,000 people were eventually resettled across eight countries, over 40,000 of them to the United States. Pittsburgh became one of the communities that took root: Bhutanese-Nepali families are today the largest refugee-origin population in Allegheny County, and their story — patience, faith, and craft carried across twenty years of statelessness — is still being written.
