Haiti Gallery.

The world's only successful slave revolution, a globally celebrated Naive art movement, and a cuisine built around remembering freedom.

A Revolution That Changed the World.

In 1791, enslaved Haitians launched an uprising that no one thought could succeed — and by 1804, they had defeated Napoleon's army and founded Haiti, the first Black republic in history and only the second independent nation in the Americas after the United States. Led first by Toussaint Louverture and then Jean-Jacques Dessalines, it remains the only slave revolt in history to end in the permanent abolition of slavery and the creation of a new state. The victory sent shockwaves through every slaveholding society in the Americas, including the United States, and directly pushed Napoleon to abandon his ambitions in North America, leading to the Louisiana Purchase the very next year.

Art, Faith, and a Table Worth Fighting For.

Haitian Vodou, born from the blending of West African spiritual traditions with Catholicism under slavery, is a structured, living religion — not the caricature Hollywood has made of it — practiced alongside Christianity by many Haitians today. Mid-20th-century Haitian painters built one of the most distinctive visual art movements in the Americas, the Haitian Naive school, filling canvases with the vivid colors and market scenes of everyday life; their work now hangs in major museums worldwide. On January 1st, Haitians mark independence with soup joumou, a rich squash soup that enslaved people were once forbidden to eat — sharing it today is a direct, edible act of remembering freedom.

Beyond the Headlines.

In 1825, France forced newly independent Haiti to pay 150 million gold francs — ten times what the U.S. paid for the entire Louisiana Territory — as "compensation" to former slaveholders for the loss of their human property, under threat of re-invasion. Haiti spent 122 years paying it off, a debt historians now estimate cost the country tens of billions of dollars in today's money — money that could have built schools, hospitals, and infrastructure instead. That history, not a lack of resilience, is central to understanding the poverty, political instability, and the devastating 2010 earthquake that followed. Haitian families rebuilding life in the U.S. carry forward a nation whose defining act was the world's greatest revolution for freedom, not the crises the headlines lead with.
