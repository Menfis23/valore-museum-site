Four thousand years of history, áo dài, water puppetry, and lacquer art.

A Civilization Older Than Its Wars.

For most Americans, Vietnam calls to mind a war that lasted twenty years. But Vietnamese civilization stretches back more than four thousand — the Đông Sơn culture was casting elaborate bronze drums by 1000 BCE, and centuries of resistance against Chinese imperial rule, from the Trưng Sisters' revolt in 40 CE onward, forged a distinct language and identity that outlasted a thousand years of occupation. The imperial capital of Huế, with its walled Citadel and royal tombs, still stands as a monument to a much longer, much older story.

Everyday Life & Art.

The áo dài — a long, fitted tunic worn over flowing trousers — is everyday dress, not costume, seen on schoolgirls cycling to class and office workers alike. In the rice paddies of the Red River Delta, farmers once invented múa rối nước, water puppetry, performing folk tales on a stage of flooded water; the tradition is still performed today. Vietnamese lacquerware, sơn mài, builds up dozens of layers of resin and eggshell or mother-of-pearl by hand, and dishes like phở and bánh mì — themselves the product of Vietnamese cooks reworking foreign ingredients into something entirely their own — are eaten on plastic stools at sidewalk stalls every single day.

Beyond the Headlines.

Vietnam developed its own script, chữ Nôm, to write its spoken language using adapted Chinese characters centuries before the Latin-based alphabet used today. Despite a millennium of Chinese rule and a century of French colonization, Vietnamese language and identity never dissolved into either. Today Vietnam is one of Asia's fastest-growing economies and a major global manufacturing and technology hub, with a vast diaspora that has rebuilt community life — and cuisine — in cities across the world.
