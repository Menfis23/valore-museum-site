Ukraine Gallery.

Pysanky, vyshyvanka, and a national identity centuries in the making.

Deep Roots.

Kyiv is older than Moscow — founded as the capital of Kyivan Rus' in the 9th century, a medieval state that shaped the culture and Orthodox Christianity of much of Eastern Europe. Later, the Cossacks of the Zaporizhian Sich built a self-governing military and political tradition on the steppes that Ukrainians still point to as a root of their independent identity.

Everyday Life & Art.

Pysanky — eggs decorated with wax-resist dye in intricate geometric and folk patterns — are made every spring, some designs passed down within a single family for generations. Vyshyvanka embroidery patterns identify the region they come from, still worn at weddings and celebrations. Ukrainian borscht was recognized by UNESCO for its cultural significance, and Ukraine's contemporary music scene, from folk-electronic fusion to Eurovision wins, keeps that same instinct for color and craft very much alive.

Beyond the Headlines.

In 1932–33, a man-made famine known as the Holodomor killed millions of Ukrainians — a chapter of Soviet history rarely covered in U.S. classrooms, and one central to how Ukrainians understand their own history of resilience. Ukraine's 1991 independence and its strong tradition of grassroots volunteer civil society reflect a long, often overlooked struggle to define a national identity distinct from its larger neighbor.
