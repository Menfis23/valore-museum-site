Nigeria Gallery.

Ife bronzes, adire indigo cloth, Nollywood, and 250 ethnic groups in one nation.

A Mosaic of Kingdoms.

Nigeria was never a single ancient empire — it was, and still is, a mosaic of sophisticated civilizations. The city-state of Ife produced bronze and terracotta sculpture more than 700 years ago so naturalistic that early European archaeologists refused to believe Africans had made it. The Benin Kingdom cast intricate brass plaques and royal heads for centuries — a tradition so refined that thousands of the Benin Bronzes were looted by a British expedition in 1897, and are still, today, the subject of active repatriation efforts by museums around the world.

Everyday Life & Art.

Adire — indigo-resist-dyed cloth stitched, stenciled, or painted with cassava starch before dyeing — has been made by Yoruba women for generations, its geometric and pictorial patterns often carrying names and meanings. Today Nigeria is also home to Nollywood, the world's second-largest film industry by output, and to Afrobeats, a global music movement built on the political Afrobeat legacy of Fela Kuti. At celebrations, guests often wear aso-ebi — matching fabric chosen for the occasion — turning weddings and funerals alike into seas of coordinated color.

Beyond the Headlines.

Nigeria is home to more than 250 ethnic groups and over 500 languages — one of the most linguistically diverse nations on Earth. Long before Ife or Benin, the Nok culture produced terracotta sculpture as early as 500 BCE, among the oldest known figurative art in sub-Saharan Africa. Since independence in 1960, Nigeria has grown into Africa's largest economy and most populous nation, and its global diaspora — in medicine, technology, literature, and business — carries that same density of talent and tradition into the rest of the world.
