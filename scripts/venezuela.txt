Waterfalls, Tepuis, and a Cradle of Independence.

Venezuela is home to Angel Falls (Kerepakupai Merú in the language of the Indigenous Pemón people, "waterfall of the deepest place"), the world's tallest uninterrupted waterfall at 979 meters, plunging off the sheer cliffs of Auyán-tepui in Canaima National Park — a landscape of ancient flat-topped mountains, some of the oldest exposed rock formations on Earth, found almost nowhere else. Venezuela also holds a foundational place in Latin American independence: in 1811 it became the first Spanish colony in South America to declare independence, led by Simón Bolívar, "El Libertador," whose campaigns across the continent would go on to liberate five nations.

Everyday Life & Art.

Music runs deep here: El Sistema, the free classical music education program founded in 1975 by José Antonio Abreu with just 11 students rehearsing in a parking garage, has grown into a network reaching hundreds of thousands of young Venezuelans and produced conductor Gustavo Dudamel, now one of the world's most celebrated musicians. Joropo, Venezuela's national folk music and dance, pairs the harp, the four-string cuatro, and maracas in fast, intricate rhythms danced at any celebration. Every Corpus Christi, the Afro-Venezuelan Diablos Danzantes ("Dancing Devils") don elaborate masks and red costumes to dance through coastal towns in a UNESCO-recognized tradition blending Catholic ritual with African heritage. And arepas — round grilled or fried cornmeal cakes split and filled with nearly anything — are eaten at every meal, a food so central to daily life that Venezuelans joke they'd rather go without bread than without arepas.

Beyond the Headlines.

Once one of Latin America's wealthiest nations on the strength of the world's largest proven oil reserves, Venezuela has, since around 2014, undergone economic collapse, hyperinflation, and authoritarian rule under Hugo Chávez's successor Nicolás Maduro — driving more than 7.7 million people to leave the country, the largest displacement crisis in Latin American history. Many have resettled in cities across the US, including Pittsburgh, rebuilding lives far from home. What they carry with them is not defined by that collapse alone: it is Dudamel's baton, the Diablos Danzantes' drumbeat, and a waterfall the Pemón named for its depth long before anyone else had a word for it.
